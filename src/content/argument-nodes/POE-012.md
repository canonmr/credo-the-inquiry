# POE-012 — Free Will and Natural Evil

## Plain-language statement

The free-will defense handles moral evil neatly: free creatures can do wrong, and God permits it for the sake of the greater good of freedom. Natural evil is harder, because it does not obviously arise from anyone's free choice. Two moves are available: (a) extend free will to non-human agents (Plantinga), so that natural evil is the indirect result of the misuse of angelic freedom; (b) keep free will at the human level but argue for the value of a regular natural order (Swinburne), in which the natural evils are by-products of a system that makes freedom, science, and character formation possible.

Both moves are contested. The chapter holds the weaker claim — that natural evil is consistent with theism and that free will is at least part of the story — and refuses to over-claim. The detailed theodicy is left open.

## What this argument does NOT establish

This argument does not establish that any particular natural disaster is caused by a particular fallen angel. It does not establish that the actual world's regular order is the best one God could create. It only establishes that free will can do at least part of the theodicy work for natural evil, and that the evidential force of the natural-evil argument is weaker than it first appears.
