# POE-009 — The Evidential Problem of Evil

## Plain-language statement

Even if the logical problem of evil has been dissolved, the evidential problem remains. William Rowe (1979) argued that instances of apparently gratuitous suffering (a fawn in a forest fire, a child in a torture case) make the existence of God improbable. Paul Draper (1989) argued by a comparative-likelihood method that the observed distribution of pain and pleasure is antecedently much more likely on a hypothesis of indifference than on theism. The argument is not that God and evil are logically incompatible; it is that the actual world's pattern of suffering is what we would expect from a world with no benevolent God.

The responses to the evidential problem are themselves substantive: skeptical theism, soul-making theodicies, the free-will defense applied to natural evil. The chapter takes each seriously and refuses to declare the debate settled.

## What this argument does NOT establish

This argument does not establish that God does not exist. It only establishes that the actual world's pattern of suffering is evidence that must be addressed. The theist may answer the evidence; the atheist may fail to do so. The argument is the opening move of a debate, not the closing of it.
