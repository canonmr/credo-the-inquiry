  - P2. If we have no special reason to doubt our ability to see reasons, then the absence of seen reasons is evidence of the absence of reasons.

  - P4. (Rowe's) If gratuitous suffering exists, then God does not exist (or, more carefully, no omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being exists).

strongest_objection: "Skeptical theism. If our epistemic position with respect to God's reasons is severely limited — if God could have reasons that we are not in a position to discern — then the no-seeum inference fails. We would not expect to see reasons for permitting the fawn's suffering even if there were such reasons."

strongest_response: "Skeptical theism is a serious response, but it has costs. If our ability to see reasons is severely limited, then our ability to see reasons in any domain is severely limited, including the reasons we have for theism itself. The skeptic is then free to use the same move: we cannot see why God would exist either, and theism loses its evidential support. Skeptical theism is a double-edged sword."

counter_response: "Skeptical theists are not committed to global skepticism. The skeptical move is restricted to our ability to discern God's *moral* reasons for *particular* instances of suffering. The asymmetry can be defended: our inability to see God's reasons in this domain is compatible with our ability to see reasons in others (e.g., cosmological, moral, aesthetic)."

caveat: "Adams's 'horrendous evils' move is a separate and important response. She argues that the right measure is not whether a particular instance is 'worth it' in the abstract but whether the participant's life on the whole is a great good. This shifts the debate from pointlessness-in-the-moment to integration-in-the-whole. The chapter must keep both moves distinct."

what_this_argument_does_not_establish:
  - "That any particular suffering is in fact gratuitous."
  - "That our ability to see reasons is in fact unimpaired."
  - "That the no-seeum inference is reliable in this domain."

evidential_challenge: "The argument turns on the reliability of the no-seeum inference. That is itself an empirical-cum-conceptual question. Empirical work (e.g., Hasker; the recent experimental philosophy on Rowe's cases) suggests that intuitions about the pointlessness of suffering are sensitive to framing and to background beliefs."

defeat_condition: "POE-010 is defeated if (a) skeptical theism is successful in blocking the no-seeum inference, (b) a theodicy is given that accounts for the specific cases, or (c) Adams's 'integration on the whole' move is accepted, in which case the relevant measure is no longer 'is this instance gratuitous?' but 'is the life as a whole a great good?'."

# POE-010 — Apparently Gratuitous Suffering

## Plain-language statement

We are confronted with suffering that seems pointless. A fawn trapped in a forest fire. A child tortured for no reason. If we have no special reason to doubt our ability to see the point of such suffering, then its apparent pointlessness is evidence of its real pointlessness. And if real pointlessness exists, the classical theistic God does not exist.

The argument is powerful, but it depends on the no-seeum inference — the move from 'we cannot see a reason' to 'there is no reason'. Skeptical theism challenges that move. Adams's 'horrendous evils' theodicy challenges the very measure: maybe the right question is not 'is this instance worth it?' but 'is the life as a whole a great good for the one who lives it?'. The chapter holds both moves in view.

## What this argument does NOT establish

This argument does not establish that any particular suffering is in fact gratuitous. It does not establish that our ability to see reasons is reliable in this domain. It only establishes that apparent pointlessness is a serious evidential datum that the theist must address.
