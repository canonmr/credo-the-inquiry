# What Is the Result of This Inquiry?

## 1. The closing question

Six chapters have passed: from the riddle of evil, to the question whether God speaks, whether the books carry the address, who has the right to interpret it, what the church claims about its founder, and what it claims about the believer. Now the question that has waited since the first chapter: *what is the result of all this?*

This chapter's answer will not outrun the evidence — and will not lag behind it. It will count what was established, name what remains contested, and place the decision where it actually stands. No slogans, no scoring.

## 2. The pattern: six chapters, one road

The first result is about shape. Every chapter ran the same road: the claim was named, the source was quoted, the strongest objection was spoken, the status was written in a ledger. And every chapter ended the same way — with a choice, not a proof.

The church points to its testimony: "What we have seen and heard we declare to you" (1 John 1:3). The inquiry's ledgers point to the status of that testimony: examined, not proven. The inquiry did not fail to prove; it refused to pretend. That is the pattern from chapter 1 to chapter 6 — and the pattern is the first result.

## 3. What the inquiry established

Then the definite results. The church's texts are exact and quotable: the address, the books, the interpreter, the founder, the believer's end. The historical picture is thin but real: a Galilean teacher executed under Pilate (Tacitus; Josephus); an early, public confession of resurrection (1 Cor 15:3-8). The definitions are precise: Nicea 325, the creeds, the Catechism. The tests are on record: the fruit-test (James 2:17), the admission of the stakes (1 Cor 15:19).

And just as important: what was not established is now established as knowledge. Documentation is not proof — but no one needs to guess what the church claims and what cannot be verified.

## 4. What remains contested

The boundary is clear. On one side: the facts of the claims. On the other: the truth of the claims — grace, resurrection, divinity, authority, judgment, heaven, hell. All stated exactly; all contested; all held by faith. "Faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen" (Heb 11:1). "If we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience" (Rom 8:25).

This chapter's honesty is to name the line — and not to pretend that naming it moves it.

## 5. The circle and the leap

Why can the line not be moved? Because every worldview stands on a foundation it cannot prove. The skeptic trusts reason — and cannot prove reason from outside reason. The scientist trusts method — and cannot prove method by another method. The believer trusts the address — and the address is exactly what was examined.

The difference is not proof; it is honesty. Kierkegaard names what remains: the leap. The inquiry does not make the leap; it makes it visible.

## 6. The bet that cannot be avoided

And the leap cannot be avoided. "You must wager. It is not optional. You are embarked" — Pascal. Refusing to believe is itself a bet: the skeptic who refuses the address lives as if death were the end. Every abstention is a choice of meaning made in silence.

James states the rule: "Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by their nature be decided on intellectual grounds." There is no neutral seat. The game deals every player a hand.

## 7. The address as the turning point

And that is the hinge. Every chapter pointed to one question: does the hidden God speak? The prophet named the hiddenness: "Truly, you are a God who hides himself" (Isa 45:15). The text names the door: "I am the gate" (John 10:9); "I am standing at the door, knocking" (Rev 3:20).

If the address is real, every later claim is a door that opens. If it is not, the whole inquiry is a study in the psychology of hope. The hinge cannot be settled by the inquiry. It is the point the inquiry faces — and the point the inquirer must decide.

## 8. The integrity test

Before the decision, one last test — and the inquiry offers it against itself. Did it quote what weakened it? Yes: the church's own admissions are quoted against its interest (1 Cor 15:19; CCC 1033; Lumen Gentium 16). Did it leave the strongest objections standing? Yes: Clifford, hiddenness, the scandal of the church — written in full, in every chapter. Did it publish its limits? Yes: every ledger has a not_established column; no worldview was scored.

The test is open to any reader. The inquiry does not claim to have passed; it claims to have made the test possible. Honesty of method does not prove the correctness of conclusions — it only proves that the conclusions were not concealed.

## 9. The objection: choice is not evidence

The strongest objection to the whole inquiry must be spoken: the conclusion "it is a choice" is not a result but a surrender. A leap is not a reason; a bet is not a proof. "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" — Clifford. And Pascal himself admitted the hiddenness: the God who hides fits every reading (pensée 194).

The church's answer is strong: no worldview offers a demonstration; the skeptic's position is the same choice, made in silence. But the objection remains standing: naming the choice does not prove it. This chapter does not close that debate — it leaves it open, as chapters 4, 5, and 6 left it.

## 10. The open door

The last word is not a conclusion but a door. "Enter through the narrow gate" (Matt 7:13-14). "I am standing at the door, knocking" (Rev 3:20). "Come to me, all you that are weary and are carrying heavy burdens" (Matt 11:28-30). "When you search for me, you will find me" (Jer 29:13). And the church names the seeker: "You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you" (Augustine, Confessions 1.1.1); "The desire for God is written in the human heart" (CCC 27).

The door is open — not because the inquiry proved it, but because the inquiry could not close it. The knock is waiting. The choice is the inquirer's.

## 11. The final ledger

Let us count honestly, once more, for the last time.

Established: an honest pattern; exact texts; a thin but real historical picture; precise definitions; a clear boundary between the facts of the claims and the truth of the claims; the knowledge that there is no neutral seat; the knowledge that the whole inquiry turns on one hinge.

Contested: whether "choice" is an honest result; whether the leap lands in truth; whether the believer's bet is better than the skeptic's; whether Clifford stands; whether the image of the door is honesty.

Not established: that the address is real; that any leap lands; that the inquiry has closed the door.

Seven chapters. Nineteen steps in the first, nine in each after — sixty-nine steps in all. And every step ends where it began: before a decision the evidence cannot make.

## 12. The last word: from inquiry to decision

This inquiry began with the riddle of evil and ends before a door. Between them: six chapters of examination, 69 argument steps, 101 sources, two languages. All honest; none deciding.

That is the real result: the inquiry has finished its work. It has placed every claim of the church on the table, with its sources open, its objections spoken, its limits published. What remains is not an unexamined question — it is a decision no examination can make.

The church names it in its own words: a knock, a door, an invitation to the weary. The skeptic names it in his own words: a bet that cannot be avoided. The inquiry names it the same way for both: a choice — made with open eyes, before evidence that has been weighed.

No slogans. No scoring. A door.
