# If God Already Knew, Why Create?

## 1. The skeptic's question

Before a child is born, the parent can see what is coming: the love, the laughter, and the long nights of pain. If the parent could choose, would they bring the child into the world? The skeptic's question is sharper when applied to God. If God knew, before creation, that humans and angels would fall, that wars would be fought, that children would die in fires, that natural disasters would crush cities — why create at all? Why not remain in the quiet of the divine life, untouched by suffering that did not have to happen?

The question is not rhetorical. It is the central question of this chapter. The chapter will not answer it with a slogan. It will press the question through its disambiguations, its strongest forms, and its deepest complications, and it will end, not with a resolution, but with a clearer sense of what the question is really asking.

## 2. Foreknowledge

The first move is to define *foreknowledge*. Foreknowledge is knowledge of future events or propositions concerning future events. In classical theism, God's knowledge is perfect: God knows every event that will occur, including the free choices of creatures.

The definition sounds technical. It is. The technical work matters because most of the popular pressure on the question of evil rides on confusions of vocabulary. We will keep the terms tight.

## 3. Foreknowledge vs. causation

The first confusion to clear is between foreknowledge and causation. Knowing that an event will occur does not, by itself, cause that event. My knowing that the sun will rise tomorrow does not make it rise. The relation between knowledge and event is conceptual, not causal.

This is the modest disambiguation. It does not solve the problem; it removes a confusion that prevents the problem from being properly stated. The next step is to ask what *does* connect God's knowledge to events, if not causation.

## 4. The parent analogy

The intuition that drives the question comes from a familiar case. A parent who foresees a child's suffering, and who has the power to prevent it without comparable cost, but who chooses not to, is at least morally questionable. We blame the parent. By analogy, the skeptic says, we should blame God.

The analogy is rhetorically powerful. It is also a fragile instrument. It assumes that the divine case is comparable to the parental case in the morally relevant respects. It is not clear that it is.

## 5. Why God's omniscience and omnipotence change the question

Three differences are usually raised. (a) Divine knowledge is infallible, not fallible. A parent might be wrong; God is not. (b) Divine purposes may be eschatological and global, not local. A parent acts within a small horizon; God, if God exists, acts within an eternal horizon. (c) Divine resources are unlimited, not limited. A parent is constrained; God is not.

These differences do not, by themselves, dissolve the moral question. A parent who knew for certain a child would suffer and could prevent it and chose not to is, on one reading, *more* blameworthy, not less. The disanalogies establish only that the analogy is suggestive, not decisive. The deeper work begins.

## 6. Permission vs. intention

The next move is to distinguish permission from intention. For an agent to permit an event is not the same as for the agent to intend the event. A parent who allows a child to fail an exam, hoping the child will learn, intends the learning and permits the failure. The two are distinct.

The Catholic tradition uses this distinction directly. CCC §311 says: "God is in no way, directly or indirectly, the cause of moral evil. He permits it, however, because he respects the freedom of his creatures and, mysteriously, knows how to derive good from it." The word "mysteriously" is doing work. The Catechism is not claiming to have a full theodicy; it is naming a conceptual distinction and then holding the deeper question open.

The conceptual move does not solve the problem. It only makes the question more precise: if God permits evil, *what reason could possibly justify the permission?*

## 7. The logical problem of evil

The question gets sharper when we ask whether God and evil are even *logically* compatible. J. L. Mackie argued in 1955 that the three propositions (God is omnipotent, God is wholly good, evil exists) are inconsistent, once we add two "quasi-logical" premises: a good thing eliminates evil as far as it can, and an omnipotent thing has no limits to what it can do. From these, an all-good omnipotent God would eliminate all evil, contradicting the existence of evil. The conclusion was atheism.

The argument had a powerful intuitive force. It was, however, *defeated* as a logical problem by Alvin Plantinga in 1977. Plantinga showed that the two added premises can be denied. A good thing might have reason to allow evil for the sake of a greater good, including the existence of free creatures who can love and choose well. And an omnipotent thing has no limits to what is *logically* possible, but it has no power to do what is logically impossible.

The most important thing to notice is the kind of move Plantinga makes. It is a *defense*, not a *theodicy*. A defense shows that two propositions are logically compatible. A theodicy tries to identify God's actual reasons. Plantinga's argument does the first; it does not do the second. The logical problem of evil, in the strong form Mackie proposed, fails. The deeper question — what God's actual reasons are — remains.

## 8. The evidential problem of evil

The contemporary form of the argument from evil is not the logical problem. It is the evidential problem. William Rowe argued in 1979 that instances of apparently gratuitous suffering (a fawn trapped in a forest fire, a child tortured for no apparent reason) make the existence of God improbable. Paul Draper argued in 1989 that the observed distribution of pain and pleasure is antecedently much more likely on a "hypothesis of indifference" than on theism.

The argument is not that God and evil are logically incompatible. It is that the actual world's pattern of suffering is what we would expect from a world with no benevolent God. This is a more serious challenge than the logical problem, because it does not depend on hidden premises that can be denied. It depends on the *appearance* of pointlessness in the actual world.

## 9. Could God have created a better world?

The natural reply is to ask whether God could have created a better world. The Catholic tradition explicitly opens this question. CCC §310 says: "With infinite power God could always create something better. But with infinite wisdom and goodness God freely willed to create a world 'in a state of journeying' toward its ultimate perfection."

This is an important concession. The Catholic tradition does not claim that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. It claims that God had a reason for creating this world rather than a better one, and that the reason may be one we cannot fully see in this life. The work of theodicy is to say what such a reason could be.

## 10. The strongest theistic response

The strongest theistic response to the evidential problem is a cluster of moves, not a single one.

The first move is the free-will defense, extended to natural evil by appeal either to non-human free agents (Plantinga) or to the value of a regular natural order in which free creatures can develop (Swinburne). The second is the soul-making theodicy (Hick), which argues that the natural evils are the soil in which character is formed. The third is Marilyn McCord Adams's "horrendous evils" move, which shifts the question from "is this instance worth it?" to "is the participant's life, on the whole, a great good?" The fourth is skeptical theism (Bergmann), which argues that the move from "we cannot see a reason" to "there is no reason" is illegitimate, given the limits of a finite mind in relation to an infinite one.

Each of these moves is contested. Each has known costs. The chapter does not endorse any one of them as decisive. It only notes that the evidential problem, while serious, is not the last word.

## 11. The strongest objection to that response

The strongest objection to the theistic response is that it is a *defense*, not a *theodicy*. The moves above establish that God and evil are *compatible* in various ways. They do not establish that the *actual* world's distribution of evil is the one we should expect from a good God. The skeptic is not asking "could God coexist with evil?" but "why this much evil, distributed in this way, to these particular creatures?"

The theist's reply is that the eschatological vision, when it comes, will be more informative than the present epistemic situation. CCC §314 says: "Only at the end, when our partial knowledge ceases, when we see God 'face to face', will we fully know the ways by which — even through the dramas of evil and sin — God has guided his creation to that definitive sabbath rest for which he created heaven and earth."

This is a theological claim. It cannot be verified from the present epistemic position. The chapter marks it as faith, not as evidence.

## 12. The Catholic treatment

The Catholic treatment of the problem of evil is not a solution. It is a reframing. The Catechism (§§309–314) places the problem within a theological frame: a God who freely creates a world in a state of journeying toward its ultimate perfection (§310); a God who is in no way the cause of moral evil but permits it because he respects creaturely freedom and "mysteriously" knows how to derive good from it (§311); a God who, in his almighty providence, is able to bring a greater good from the consequences of evil, even moral evil, the cross and resurrection being the central example (§312); and a God whose ways of providence are not fully available to us in this life, but will be known only at the eschatological vision "face to face" (§314).

The reframing is honest. It does not pretend to have a complete theodicy. It claims only that the Catholic tradition has a way of holding the problem of evil without dissolving it, and that the holding is honest about the present-life epistemic limits.

The project constitution explicitly forbids two over-claims that the Catholic treatment does *not* support: (a) that fallen angels empirically explain particular natural disasters; (b) that every natural evil is directly caused by a particular human sin. The chapter respects these limits.

## 13. What remains unresolved

A great deal remains unresolved.

The logical problem of evil has been dissolved by Plantinga, but the dissolution is a defense, not a theodicy. The evidential problem of evil is live; the no-seeum inference that drives it is contested but not refuted. The free-will defense, even in its extended form, is at best part of the story for natural evil. Skeptical theism blocks one inference but opens another. The Catholic reframing is honest about what it does and does not know, and it does not pretend to a full theodicy.

The chapter does not declare the debate settled. The debate is not settled. The chapter's work is to leave the reader with a clearer sense of what the question is really asking, and what would count as an answer.

## 14. Where the question leads next

The question that opens this chapter — *if God already knew, why create?* — has moved. We began with the question of foreknowledge. We moved through the parent analogy, the permission/intention distinction, the logical and evidential problems, the comparative question of a better world, and the Catholic reframing. We have not arrived at a final answer.

What we have arrived at is a clearer sense of the question's structure. The question is no longer "did God know?" but "what reasons could justify creating and permitting a world like this?". That is the deeper problem. It is the problem of theodicy, the problem of God's actual reasons.

The next chapter will not solve the problem of theodicy. It will turn to the question of *revelation*: if God has reasons we cannot see in this life, on what grounds would we expect God to make those reasons at least partially visible? That is a different question. It is the question of whether the God of classical theism would, or could, speak.
