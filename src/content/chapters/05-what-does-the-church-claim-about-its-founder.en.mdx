# What Does the Church Claim About Its Founder?

## 1. The question that remains

Chapter four ended at a claim: the church has authority — and if it has authority, its words matter. Now their content must be examined. The church speaks about three things: its founder, its worship, and itself. The first is the greatest: *who is Jesus Christ?* If the address of God is real, the church says, it became a person — Jesus. This chapter examines the claim about that person: what the church says, what can be checked, and what cannot. As always: no slogans, only examination.

## 2. The central claim: the address made flesh

Every community stands on a claim. The church's claim is precise: Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God. "You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God" — so Peter confesses, and Jesus calls the confession a revelation from the Father (Matthew 16:16-17). "And the Word became flesh and lived among us" (John 1:14). "In these last days he has spoken to us by a Son" (Hebrews 1:2).

In the language of this inquiry: the address of God — the theme of every chapter — is claimed to have become a person. The claim is breathtaking. And precisely because it is breathtaking, it must be examined: first its historical anchors, then its theological content, then its testing.

## 3. The historical Jesus: what the records allow

What can history establish without faith? A thin but real portrait. Tacitus, a hostile Roman writing c. 116: "Christus... suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus." Josephus, c. 93: a teacher named Jesus drew followers and was condemned to the cross by Pilate — in a passage whose authenticity scholars partly dispute. And the church's own documents claim eyewitnesses: "We declare to you what we have seen and heard" (1 John 1:3).

The execution is as well attested as any event of antiquity. The confession — that this man is the Christ — is not attested by history. History reaches the foot of the cross and stops.

## 4. The resurrection: the founding claim

The church was not founded on a book — it was founded on a proclamation: "God raised him up, having freed him from death" (Acts 2:24). And the proclamation is carried by an early creed, quoted by Paul around 55: "that Christ died for our sins... that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day... and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve... then to more than five hundred brothers and sisters at one time... then to James, then to all the apostles" (1 Corinthians 15:3-7).

The creed is early — scholars date it to within a few years of the crucifixion. It is public. It is foundational. What it is not is proven: a claim that is old and public is still a claim. The appearances could be appearances of a risen Lord — or the visions of a community in grief. The evidence fits both readings. That is not a defect of the claim; it is its true status.

## 5. The divinity of Christ: Nicaea's answer

The church's claim about its founder does not stop at the resurrection. "True God from true God, begotten, not made, of one substance with the Father" — so defined the Council of Nicaea in 325. The word *homoousios*, "of one substance", was chosen deliberately to exclude the position of Arius, who taught that Christ was a creature, the first and greatest but not God.

Two facts must be distinguished. The first: the definition was made in history — a real controversy, real bishops, real imperial politics (Constantine convened and enforced the council). The second: its content is not verifiable. No method can check whether Christ is "of one substance with the Father". Nicaea's answer is a definition of faith — made in history, about what is beyond history.

## 6. The sacraments: what the church claims they do

The church claims that its worship does what no symbol can do. Baptism: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them" (Matthew 28:19) — and the Catechism: "Through Baptism we are freed from sin and reborn as sons of God" (CCC 1213). The Eucharist: "Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you" (John 6:53) — "This is my body... this cup... is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:19-20) — and the Catechism: the Eucharist is "the source and summit of the Christian life" (CCC 1324).

The claims are precise. And they are unverifiable: grace cannot be measured, new birth cannot be observed. What can be examined is the claim itself — and whether it is a promise made on Christ's authority or an assertion of the party being tested. That is the honest form of the sacramental question.

## 7. The marks of the church: one, holy, catholic, apostolic

The church also speaks about itself. "We believe in one holy catholic and apostolic Church" — so the creed of 381. Four words, four claims.

Now the facts: the visible church is divided into thousands of denominations; its history contains crusades, inquisitions, and scandals. The "one" church is not visibly one; the "holy" church is not visibly holy. The church's answer: the marks describe its identity from Christ, not the performance of its members. The observer's answer: the marks are contradicted by the facts. Both readings are held honestly. Deciding between them is deciding what the church is.

## 8. History and faith: two methods, one person

The same person is examined by two methods. History verifies the man and the execution, dates the earliest proclamation, and stops at the foot of the cross. Faith confesses what history cannot show: "You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God."

The two do not contradict — they differ in reach. But that is not a truce; it marks exactly where the decision lies. The honest question is not "who uses no interpretation?" — everyone does. It is: whose interpretation fits more, explains more, costs more? And that question is not settled by evidence; it is settled by the person.

## 9. The circle returns: the claims presuppose the address

Every claim of this chapter stands on a deeper one. Christ is the Word made flesh — the address in person. But the address was not proven in chapter 2; the books were not proven in chapter 3; the interpreter was not proven in chapter 4. The church's claims stand on all three.

The believer inhabits this structure: it is what faith is. The outsider sees a circle. And every worldview is a circle at its base: the skeptic's base is the reliability of reason; the scientist's is the reliability of method. What differs is honesty. This inquiry has been honest from the first chapter: the foundations are choices. The choice between circles is the existential question.

## 10. The strongest objection

The strongest objection must be spoken without comfort: the church's claims about Christ are not proven — they are chosen. History gives a crucified teacher; faith confesses the risen Son of God; both readings fit the same evidence. "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" — Clifford, 1877.

The confession exceeds the evidence. That is not an insult to the claim — it is its honest status. The believer's answer is equally honest: the exceeding is the point of the claim — "For God so loved the world that he gave his only Son" (John 3:16) — not its flaw. And the debate between Clifford and the believer — should belief ever exceed evidence? — is a living philosophical question, not a settled one. This chapter does not close it.

## 11. What remains unanswered

Let us count honestly.

What can be said: the church's claim about its founder is precise and textual; history establishes a thin but real portrait; the resurrection creed is early, public, and foundational; Nicaea defined the divinity of Christ with a word whose content no method can verify; the sacramental and ecclesial claims are exactly stated — and exactly unverifiable. What cannot be said: that the claims are true. And what cannot be said either: that they are false.

The ledger of this chapter: established — the texts, the portrait, the early creed, the definitions. Contested — the confession, the resurrection, the divinity, the sacraments, the marks. Not established — every claim of faith. Five chapters in, the pattern is consistent: the inquiry has examined everything and proven nothing — because the decisions were never evidence, but choices in the presence of evidence.

## 12. Where this question leads

If the church's claims about its founder are a choice, then the church's claims about the believer are the same kind of choice — and they are the next question. The church says: the address that became a person in Christ does not stop there. It continues — in baptism, in the Eucharist, in the life of the believer, to its end. *What does the church claim happens to the believer?*

That is the question of the *life* the church offers: grace, conversion, and the end of the believer — salvation, judgment, eternal life. That is the next chapter's question. As always, we will not walk faster than the evidence — but we will not stop because the evidence is hard.
