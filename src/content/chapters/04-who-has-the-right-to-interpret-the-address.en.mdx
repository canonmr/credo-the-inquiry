# Who Has the Right to Interpret the Address?

## 1. The question that remains

Chapter three ended at a wall: the books that carry the address were chosen and transmitted by a community — and that community is the church. The question of the books became the question of the church. And the question of the church is a question of *authority*: who has the right to say "this is God's address, and this is what it means"?

The question can be split into three. First, *what* the church claims — what is the content of its claim to authority? Second, *how* it grounds that claim — in Christ, in the apostles, in its history? Third, *whether* the claim can be examined — or only accepted. This chapter traces all three. As always: no slogans, only examination.

## 2. What the church claims

We begin with the content of the claim. The church claims to have received the authority to bind and loose directly from Christ. Three texts carry this claim. "You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church" — and "I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 16:18-19). "Whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven" (Matthew 18:18) — said to the whole community of disciples. And to the apostles in the upper room: "If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them" (John 20:23).

Note what has happened: the claim is not groundless. It is rooted in texts that both sides — Catholic and Protestant — recognize as Scripture. The debate is not *whether the texts exist*, but *how they are read*. And that is already the shape of the whole chapter: the question of authority begins in the text, and the text must be interpreted.

## 3. Apostolic succession: a traceable history

The church grounds its authority not only in texts, but in a chain: the bishops of today sit in the seats of the apostles. Dei Verbum 8 says the Gospel is preserved "by an unending succession of preachers" — and that the apostles appointed successors. Irenaeus, writing around 180, drew up the very list: from Peter and Paul down to his own day, in the church of Rome — which, he argued, all churches must agree with because of its apostolic origin.

Here the claim must be divided. The *historical* side is real: the succession lists exist, and for old sees they can be traced. But the *theological* side — that the chain guarantees truth — is a claim of faith, not a fact of history. Any two-thousand-year-old organization can draw up its line of officeholders; the chain proves continuity, not truth. Irenaeus used it to show *where* the tradition is to be found. It does not yet show *that* the tradition is true.

## 4. The magisterium: the living teaching office

Now the question sharpens: who interprets? In Catholicism the answer is clear and concentrated. "The task of giving an authentic interpretation of the Word of God... has been entrusted to the living teaching office of the Church alone" (Dei Verbum 10). "The task of interpreting the Word of God authentically has been entrusted solely to the Magisterium of the Church, that is, to the Pope and to the bishops in communion with him" (Catechism 85).

A clear answer. And precisely because it is clear, it moves the question: *why* should this office be trusted? The answer "the magisterium" does not end the debate — it names the party whose claim is being tested. The next sections test it.

## 5. The circle returns

In chapter three we found a circle: the church chose the books that contain claims about the church. Chapter four finds the same circle in sharper form. The church that chose the books also claims the right to interpret them.

If the church interprets, its reading cannot be tested against the text without borrowing the church's interpretation. If every reader interprets for himself, that reading cannot be tested without borrowing the reader's authority. And there is no neutral referee between the two — every referee is one of the two. The circle is not broken from outside; it is only broken by a choice: trust the office, or trust a reading. The next two sections try both exits.

## 6. The Protestant challenge: Scripture alone

There is one sharp answer to the magisterium: no. In 1520 Luther rejected the division of "clergy and laity" as "lying and hypocrisy": all Christians are priests, and every believer can read Scripture. No office has the right to fix a binding meaning.

But there is a price to pay. "No prophecy of scripture is a matter of one's own interpretation" (2 Peter 1:20). If private interpretation is forbidden, and the magisterium is rejected — who interprets? The Protestant answer: the text itself, guided by the Spirit, tested by the community. But that answer still requires readers: two devout readers, both submissive to text and Spirit, can reach conflicting readings, and no procedure decides between them. Sola scriptura does not break the circle; it moves it from the office to the reader.

## 7. Infallibility: the claim and its history

The Catholic exit has a name: infallibility. And it is a very specific claim. The Pope cannot err only when he teaches *ex cathedra* — as pastor and teacher of all the faithful, definitively, on faith or morals — and that definition is "irreformable of themselves, and not by reason of the consent of the Church" (Pastor Aeternus 4). The college of bishops with the Pope shares this gift under defined circumstances (Lumen Gentium 25; Catechism 891).

Now history. In 681, the Third Council of Constantinople — recognized by Catholics as ecumenical — condemned Pope Honorius I as a heretic. Defenders answer: Honorius did not teach ex cathedra. The objection answers: the conditions were defined in 1870, and every historical case is acquitted by redefinition. That is the honest shape of the debate: the claim is precisely defined — and precisely because it is precise, it can be tested against the magisterium's own history. That test is unfinished.

## 8. The test of fruits

If no standard is neutral, perhaps there is one that Jesus himself gave: "You will know them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:16). Every good tree bears good fruit.

But applied to the church, the criterion becomes ambiguous. The Catholic church has two thousand years of saints and works of charity. Other communities have their piety and their sacrifice. There is no neutral scale that weighs "whose fruits are better" without borrowing the standard of one of the parties. The test can screen out what is consistently rotten — abuse, oppression, systematic lying. It cannot decide between trees that are equally fruitful.

## 9. Authority as service

There remains one internal measure, and it is the most striking. Jesus himself gave the church the measure of its own authority: "The kings of the Gentiles lord it over them... But not so with you... the leader must become like one who serves" (Luke 22:25-27). And faith is "the human response to God who reveals" (Catechism 143) — a response that cannot be forced.

This reframes the question. The claim of authority is judged by its own founder's criterion: does this authority serve, or does it rule? No argument can decide for us — the decision remains a choice. But the choice is framed: it is a choice in the presence of evidence, weighed with open eyes, under the measure of service.

## 10. The strongest objection

The strongest objection must be spoken clearly: the church's claim cannot be tested. Scripture? Its interpretation is determined by the magisterium. History? Its judgment is disputed. Fruits? The scale is held by the party being tested. "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" — Clifford's principle, 1877.

If every test borrows the claim itself, then the claim has not passed any test. That does not prove the claim false. It proves that the claim is accepted through choice — and that honesty demands calling it a choice, not evidence. The strongest response: every knowledge system borrows standards; science borrows its methods, law its constitution. But the borrowing that can be corrected by evidence is one thing; the borrowing that declares itself irreformable is another. The debate between these two — revisable trust and definitive authority — is alive, and this chapter does not close it.

## 11. What remains unanswered

Let us count honestly.

What can be said: the church's claim is grounded in texts acknowledged by both sides; its succession is partly traceable; its answer to the question of interpretation is clear; its history contains cases that its own definitions must absorb. What cannot be said: that the claim is proven — and it cannot be said that it is disproven. The circle of interpretation is real; the Protestant exit moves it; the infallibility exit defines it; the tests of fruits and service frame the choice without deciding it.

This chapter does not declare the magisterium true or false. It declares something simpler and harder: that the claim of authority is a real claim, with real texts, a real history — and a real question that cannot be dissolved by slogans. The decision between trust and mistrust is a decision: honest, personal, and made in the presence of evidence.

## 12. Where this question leads

If the church has authority, then its words matter — including its words about itself: about who Christ is, about what the sacraments do, about what happens to the believer. And if its words about itself are tested, they are tested against the same criteria of this inquiry: texts, history, reason — and the fruits of what the church actually teaches.

That is the question of the *claims of the church*: the church's teaching about its founder, its worship, and the life it offers. That is the next chapter's question. As always, we will not walk faster than the evidence — but we will not stop because the evidence is hard.
