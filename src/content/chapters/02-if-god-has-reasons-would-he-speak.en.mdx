# If God Has Reasons, Would He Speak?

## 1. The question that remains

The first chapter ended with a question left open. If God has reasons for permitting evil — reasons we cannot see in this life — on what grounds could we expect those reasons to become at least partially visible? That is the question of *revelation*: would God speak, or can God speak?

The question can be read in two ways. The first is purely philosophical. *Can* God speak? Is communication between God and human beings possible at all? The second is historical and concrete. If God speaks, *where* do we find the speech — in the heart, in nature, in Scripture, in the Church? This chapter pursues both readings. It does not end with a proof that God speaks; it ends with a clearer sense of what would count as proof.

## 2. Divine hiddenness

We begin with a fact nearly every honest person feels: God is *not* present in the way the objects around us are present. Believer and atheist alike live in a world that seems able to proceed without God. The sky does not split; no voice comes from the clouds; no fire descends. This is what is called *divine hiddenness*. The prophet Isaiah even calls God "a God who hides himself" (Isaiah 45:15).

Notice what has not yet been said. Hiddenness is not yet absence. A friend sitting silent across the room is still there; the silence does not erase the presence. The problem is just here: can that silence be distinguished from absence? And if it cannot be distinguished, may the seeker still hope?

## 3. Two kinds of revelation

To move forward we need two terms that are often conflated. *Natural revelation*: what can be known about God from creation, by reason — the order of nature, existence itself, the moral sense within the heart. *Special revelation*: the events and words in history that are claimed to be God's address — the prophets, Christ, Scripture. The Catechism (CCC 50) sharply distinguishes these two "orders of knowledge": what reason can reach from God's works, and what only God's self-address can give.

The distinction matters. Without it we would demand from natural revelation what only special revelation could give, and vice versa. The distinction is also honest: it does not claim that nature speaks as clearly as the pages of Scripture, nor that Scripture is as unambiguous as nature.

## 4. Natural revelation: creation as language

Does creation speak? The Christian tradition answers yes, carefully. Paul writes that "ever since the creation of the world his eternal power and divine nature, invisible though they are, have been understood and seen through the things he has made" (Romans 1:20). Thomas Aquinas argues that God's existence can be demonstrated from his effects in nature — the path known as the "five ways" (Summa Theologiae I, q. 2). The Catechism (CCC 36) follows both: human reason *is capable* of knowing that a personal God exists.

But notice the limits of this claim. Natural revelation, in its strongest form, points to God's *existence* — not to God's particular will, not to his plan for this world, not to his reasons for permitting evil. It is like a nameplate at a gate: it tells you someone lives there, but not why the door has been left closed.

## 5. Special revelation: God who addresses us in history

Here the Christian claim becomes concrete and, therefore, vulnerable. Hebrews 1:1-2 sums it up: "Long ago God spoke to our ancestors in many and various ways by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by a Son." Vatican II, Dei Verbum 2, calls it the "unveiling of the mystery of his will": the invisible God, out of the abundance of his love, "speaks to men as friends".

The claim is bold and difficult. It claims that the invisible has *already* appeared — in the history of a people, in the words of prophets, in the life of Jesus. If true, this is the most direct answer to the first chapter: God does not merely have reasons; God has begun to show them. If false, it is the greatest lie in history. There is no comfortable middle ground.

## 6. Can God speak?

Before assessing that claim, we must test something more basic: *can* God speak? There is a serious philosophical objection: language is an instrument of embodied creatures; God is not embodied; therefore God cannot speak. The objection, so far, is unproven. Our words were indeed coined in an embodied world, but their meaning does not depend entirely on the body: we speak of justice, of numbers, of love — things that are not embodied. There is no evident contradiction in the idea that God conveys truth to human minds — whether through inward promptings, through interpreted events, or through inspired human beings.

This is not to say special revelation is easy. It remains difficult — but the difficulty lies not in "impossible" but in "how do we know this is not just a human claim?"

## 7. The hiddenness argument

The question now becomes: if God truly wants to be known and loved, why not show himself clearly? J. L. Schellenberg (1993) gave the sharpest form to this objection. If God exists and is perfectly loving, God must desire a personal relationship with every capable creature; and a God who desires that would make himself sufficiently visible that sincere seekers find him. But the facts are otherwise: there are sincere seekers who find nothing — *nonresistant nonbelief*. Therefore, Schellenberg concludes, a perfectly loving God does not exist.

Notice the strength of the argument. It does not demand proof of absence; it uses the reality of experience — people who honestly seek and fail. It also demands something specific from the Christian side: not merely that God *could* be hidden, but that hiddenness is *compatible with perfect love*. This is the strongest form of this chapter's question.

## 8. The natural reply: love that invites, not forces

What can be answered? The first reply moves on the notion of love. Perfect love, the defenders say, does not force its presence. Forced presence violates freedom; and freedom is the condition of love itself — as we already saw in the free-will defense of the first chapter. A God who blazed his face with unbearable light would win worship, not love. Hiddenness, on this reading, is the space in which freedom grows.

This is not merely a modern theory. Isaiah 45:15 calls God "a God who hides himself". Pascal argues that the hiddenness is *intended*: a religion that does not confess that God is hidden is not a true religion (Pensées 194).

## 9. The deeper reply: faith as response, not as conclusion

The second reply is subtler and, in my judgment, more honest. It rejects a hidden assumption in Schellenberg's argument: that a relationship with God must begin from certainty. The Catholic tradition rejects that. The Catechism (CCC 143) says: "By faith, man completely submits his intellect and his will to God." Faith is not the conclusion of sufficient evidence; faith is a *response* — the human answer, free and whole, to God's address.

Notice what this changes. The hiddenness argument assumes that the sincere seeker must find. The Catholic reply: what is sought is not a conclusion but an address — and an address requires two parties. God has spoken; the question is whether the human being will answer. This moves the question from "why does God not show himself?" to "has God spoken, and are we listening?"

## 10. The strongest objection to that reply

The strongest objection is this: the reply is too easy. Calling hiddenness "space for freedom" and "an address requiring a response" only means something if the address *has actually occurred*. But there is the problem: the address itself cannot be independently verified. The Hindu hears an address in the Vedas; the Christian in Scripture; the atheist hears nothing. If every tradition claims a different address, "faith as response" becomes an elegant way of saying that everyone may believe what they already believe.

And Schellenberg's argument is not fully answered by either reply. There are people who not only fail to find, but fail to find *after* searching with all their hearts — and take the silence as the answer. To call them "resistant" or "contrary" is unjust, and to call them "insincere" is something we cannot know.

## 11. What remains unresolved

So let us tally honestly.

What can be said: hiddenness does not prove absence. Natural revelation yields real but limited knowledge of God — of his existence, not of his plan. Special revelation offers a concrete claim that is not independently proven. Faith as response explains how hiddenness and love can coexist — but only if the address is real. And Schellenberg's argument is not refuted by any defense in this chapter; it is softened, not killed.

This chapter does not declare the debate settled. It ends where an honest account should end: with the clear statement that "does God speak?" is a question that can be answered, but is not answered here — and that its criterion is not feeling, but something examinable.

## 12. Where the question leads

If special revelation truly occurred, it must have occurred somewhere concrete: in the books claimed to be inspired, in the community that claims to transmit them, in the confession about who Jesus is. The question of this chapter — *does God speak?* — therefore moves to the next question: *do those books truly carry that address, and can the community that transmits them be trusted?*

That is the question of Scripture and the Church. It is the question of the next chapter. As always, we will not run ahead of the evidence.
